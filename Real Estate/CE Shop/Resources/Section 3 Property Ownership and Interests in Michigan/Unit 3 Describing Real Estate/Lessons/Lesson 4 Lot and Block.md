
## Lot and Block

Have you ever seen a property described as _Lot 6 of Block 3 of the East Subdivision plat as recorded in Map Book 18, Page 11 at the Recorder of Deeds_? It is an example of a lot and block land description, a commonly used land description method. We'll learn more about this in this lesson.

## What Is a Lot and Block?

The ==🟡lot and block== system, also known as the plat reference system, describes land by referencing its plat, lot, and block. Lot and block systems are surveyed by first referencing either a rectangular government survey or a metes and bounds description. Then, the land is divided into lots with a numerical parcel designation. The block refers to the name of the subdivision under which the map is recorded.

A licensed surveyor or engineer creates the lot and block system, beginning with a subdivision plat. The surveyor/engineer divides the land into numbered (or lettered) lots and blocks, including streets, access roads, and other important features. All lot measurements and distances between points are included, making the map very detailed. When approved, the ==🟡plat map== becomes part of the legal description.

A legal description of a plat may include a plat map and lot number from a recorded subdivision. This description will include the book and page number where the map is recorded, making it easy for anyone to look up and find the precise location and dimensions of the property.

## Legal Description

A property may be described by reference to the section, block, and lot on the tax map, or a prior recorded instrument. The legal description of a property under the lot and block system may be something like:

_Lot 6 of Block 3 of the East Subdivision plat as recorded in Map Book 18, Page 11 at the Recorder of Deeds._

The more technical details of the legal description are contained in the recorded plat map, so there’s no need to include them in the deed.

A legal description of this type may be included in addition to, or in lieu of, a metes and bounds description, and, when accurate, it is generally considered by the courts as the preferable system to describe the property.
