
## Basic Property Rights

Basic property rights include the rights of:

- Disposition - the right to sell or convey the property
- Exclusion - the right to decide who may or may not access the property
- Enjoyment - the right of the owner to use and enjoy the property in any legal manner
- Possession - the right to occupy the property in privacy, which belongs to the legal title holder
- Control - the owner controls the use of the property
## Another Right in the Bundle

The bundle of rights is a common term that addresses the complexities of property ownership rights and helps to explain how a property can be “owned” by multiple parties at the same time. Because the rights can be separated, an owner can transfer one of them, such as the right to occupy the property through a lease, and still keep the rest.

Along with property ownership, rights are granted to use the land’s physical components. These are known as surface, subsurface, and air rights. Surface rights include both land and water rights. Water rights are yet another stick in the bundle.

## Surface, Subsurface, and Air Rights

Along with property ownership, rights are granted to use the land’s physical components. These are known as surface, subsurface, and air rights. Surface rights include both land and water rights. Subsurface rights pertain to the right to use underground resources such as natural gas and minerals. These are often referred to as mineral rights. Air rights involve the right to use the open space above buildings up to a height established by law. The owner may separate each of these rights from the land by selling or conveying the right to another. For instance, landowners sometimes sell or lease mineral rights and retain surface and air rights.

Each of these rights is one part of the bundle of rights, so selling or leasing one of them doesn’t affect the others.

## Water Rights: Types and Processes

Water rights are tied to the land that touches the water. There are two primary types of surface water rights: riparian rights, for land along flowing water such as rivers and streams, and littoral rights, for land along lakes, oceans, and other bodies of water that don’t flow in one direction like a river.

Natural processes can change a waterfront owner’s land. Accretion is the process by which water carries rock, sand, and soil and builds up land, and alluvion is the new land deposited by accretion. Erosion is the gradual loss of land, reliction is the gradual receding of water that uncovers new land, and avulsion is a sudden loss of land, usually from a flood or a shift in a river’s course.

## Prior Appropriation

Under the doctrine of prior appropriation, water rights go to the first individual or entity to put water from a source to beneficial use, such as agriculture or municipal water supplies. The state grants these rights based on use, not on whether the property touches the water, unlike riparian and littoral rights. The doctrine developed in the water-scarce western states.

Prior appropriation water rights are generally separate from land ownership and can be sold, although in some states they stay attached to the land of the permit holder. In a shortage, the most recent permit holders are cut off first, and the first permit holder is ensured its full water right.
