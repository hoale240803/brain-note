**The Duty of Disclosure**

Licensees must disclose to their clients any fact known to them that relates to the transaction.

Example

1. Maurice owns a home on a nice-sized lot. He asks Linda to represent him in the sale of the property. Linda signs a listing agreement with him, and afterward, she notices that there’s an extra tax lot on the property and that it could be subdivided and possibly sold for a lot more. Linda’s brother is a builder and is always looking for development opportunities. This would be perfect for him. But if Linda brings him in as a buyer, she would have to disclose to Maurice two things: the buyer is her brother, and her brother intends to make a profit on the property by carving it into two parcels and developing them. Even more importantly, Linda needs to help the client understand that the property may be worth more than it’s listed for if indeed it can be divided and developed. If Linda fails in this duty to disclose, she would also be failing in the duty of loyalty to her client.

**Disclosure to Non-Clients**

Licensees also may owe the duty of disclosure to other parties, not just the licensee’s clients. However, a licensee doesn’t have to disclose everything, only material facts, and a licensee must not disclose confidential information about a client to others.

Examples

1. Jake, a buyer, asked Robin, the seller’s agent, whether the property’s roof had ever been repaired. If Robin knows of a repair, she would have to disclose it. Licensees are required to disclose all material facts to all parties. Sometimes the duty of disclosure may conflict with the duty of obedience.
2. Tam, a seller, asks his prospective agent, Fiona, not to mention the leaky basement. Fiona must either convince Tam to disclose or not take the listing. Under no circumstance can Fiona take the listing and then disclose anyway, or take the listing and fail to disclose a material fact. Both would be violations of the law.
