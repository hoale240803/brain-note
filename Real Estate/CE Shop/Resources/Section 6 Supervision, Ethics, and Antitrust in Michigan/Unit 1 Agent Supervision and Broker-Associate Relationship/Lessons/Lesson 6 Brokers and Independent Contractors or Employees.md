
## Broker-Independent Contractor or Employee Agreement

A big part of brokerage management is **people management**. Brokers and licensees must understand how workers are classified at both the federal and state levels, as well as the implications of treating an independent contractor as an employee, and vice versa.

### Independent Contractor or Employee

One of the more important considerations of employment is whether you’ll be operating as a broker’s employee or as an independent contractor. If you’re an employee, you’re paid by salary (weekly, monthly, hourly, etc.). You put in your time, and you’re paid for that time. Independent contractors are paid for their results. As an independent contractor, you’d be compensated based on the number and type of transactions you close.

### Misclassification


> [!danger] 
It is extremely important to properly classify all workers. **Misclassification can result in civil penalties.**


> [!warning] 
Best practice is to **presume they are employees** and follow IRS guidelines to determine if they should, instead, be classified as independent contractors or non-employees.

## Differentiating a Non-Employee from an Employee

Because a broker has supervisory responsibility for all associated licensees, these licensees are **generally considered employees for the purposes of licensing law**, no matter whether they’re independent contractors or employees. However, the IRS makes a clear distinction.

![[Pasted image 20261125104617.png]]

>[!danger] **Brokers must withhold income and Social Security taxes from an employee’s earnings.**

There’s no requirement to do this if the licensee is an independent contractor (you’ll have to withhold them for yourself or pay them in the long run anyway). The broker must also pay unemployment compensation on wages paid to employees and pay for certain benefits for employees.

>[!warning] Keep in mind that if you work as an independent contractor, your brokerage firm will issue a **1099** to you at the end of the year to identify your commissions earned; the firm issues **W-2** forms to employees.

> [!warning] These forms identify the employee’s gross pay and all withholding for the year.

## Qualifying as an Independent Contractor

Many licensees work as independent contractors, and **the IRS decides who qualifies**. Whether you are a salesperson working with a broker or a broker working with associated licensees, the same lines apply to both sides of the relationship.

For the licensee, qualifying as an independent contractor matters because an independent contractor **can deduct certain business expenses**. For the broker, getting it right matters just as much, since treating someone as an independent contractor when the IRS would see an employee can create **employment tax liability for the brokerage**.


> [!warning]
To qualify, the licensee and the broker must **make sure not to cross lines** that would, in the eyes of the IRS, make the licensee an employee.

### What the broker can’t do

The broker must provide the oversight that license law requires, and that oversight has limits. The broker can’t:


> [!NOTE] 
> - **Dictate** the licensee’s hours
> - **Dictate** the licensee’s methods, beyond ensuring the licensee follows 
> - brokerage policies and all applicable laws
> - **Pay** the licensee for hours worked, only for results
> - **Pay** the licensee’s business expenses
> - **Fix** the licensee’s location, unless state license laws require it

### What the licensee must have


> [!NOTE] 
> - **A current real estate license**
> - **A written contract** with the broker stating that the licensee will not be
> -  treated as an employee for federal tax purposes
> - **At least 90% of income** coming from production, not hours worked

![[Pasted image 20261125105817.png]]


> [!NOTE] Note: The 90% guideline is one commonly cited benchmark. The IRS applies a multi-factor test to determine independent contractor vs. employee status, and meeting this threshold alone does not guarantee classification as an independent contractor. Whether you are the licensee or the broker, consult a tax professional for guidance specific to your situation.
