
## Interior Environmental Hazards

Let’s learn about some of the possible toxins that may be found inside a home. While not all of these will be a factor in every home, many homes have some concerns, and the more you know now, the more prepared you'll be to help your clients address them before or after the sale.

## Radon

Like asbestos, radon has natural origins. It’s a colorless, odorless, radioactive gas that’s released when uranium decays. It can end up in soil and well water. It’s not a problem walking around outside, because it’s released into the atmosphere. But when it enters a home, which is closed up, for the most part, it can cause health problems. The gas breaks down into radioactive particles that stay behind as an unwelcome visitor. The particles, when breathed, can lead to lung cancer. In fact, it’s the second-leading cause of lung cancer.

Radon enters the home through the cracks and crevices of the building—floor joints, floor drains, cracks in the concrete, the sump pump opening, wall joints, and porous walls. It can walk in your front door on the shoes of a traveling salesperson, speaking of unwelcome visitors! Radon becomes a health concern when it exceeds a specific level—4 picocuries per liter of air, in case you’re ever on Jeopardy!—but any level of radon over 0 is considered unsafe. The good news is that it’s fairly easy to lower radon levels, but first you have to know it’s there.

Homeowners can purchase a radon test kit and set it up where it won't be disturbed. The place with the highest levels will likely be the lowest level of the house, so one definitely should be placed there. After a few days, they have to be sent to a laboratory for analysis. Or you can have a radon technician both place and pick up the test, and then send it in for you.

If the lab test indicates that radon levels are above 4 picocuries, the homeowner can install a system that vents and filters the radon from the home. Mitigation systems may cost anywhere from a few hundred to a few thousand dollars. It's also helpful to seal any cracks or spaces in the concrete slab, foundation walls, or chimneys. Homes with dirt crawl spaces may require installation of a special plastic liner over the dirt to prevent radon from leaching into the home.

## Asbestos

Some homes, particularly older homes, have asbestos tiles or flooring, or asbestos wrapping around ducts. If inspectors see something they think may be asbestos, they’ll point it out. This doesn’t mean that anything needs to be done about it, but the buyer should be aware that asbestos exists.

Asbestos tends not to be a problem unless it’s disturbed. The airborne particles cause issues, as they lodge in lung tissue and can lead to lung cancer and other problems. When asbestos is found, it’s best to leave it alone, or use a professional trained in asbestos remediation to remove it. Newer homes don’t generally contain asbestos.

Asbestos was frequently used in manufacturing because of its fire-retardant and insulating qualities, and because it was strong. However, due to the health consequences of asbestos, it’s no longer used. In addition, some buildings containing asbestos, if not disposed of properly, could contaminate the soil, which may track into even new buildings.

## Mold

Mold is a common toxin found in new and older homes. It can appear wherever there is moisture and a lack of air circulation, such as unvented attics and behind laminated walls in bathrooms. If mold is suspected, an inspector will recommend testing. Mold is dangerous because it can cause respiratory problems at high levels, especially for sensitive individuals. 

Moisture issues should be addressed right away. Because mold needs moisture to grow, if there’s a leak of any size, it should be fixed immediately. Mold growth should also be addressed right away. Small mold issues may be treatable by homeowners. Larger issues should be handled by professionals.
